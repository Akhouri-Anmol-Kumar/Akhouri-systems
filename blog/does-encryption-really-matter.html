Does Encryption Really Matter?

An honest answer for people who don’t think of themselves as “security people”.

2026-10-25 · 6 min · By Akhouri Anmol Kumar

Short answer: yes, but not for the reason most people think. Encryption isn’t about hiding secrets. It’s about making sure that the worst day of your digital life (a stolen laptop, a leaked password, a curious stranger on your PC) stays an inconvenience instead of a disaster.

Ask ten people whether they need encryption and you’ll hear the same two answers. The first: “I have nothing to hide.” The second: “That’s for hackers and governments.”

Both answers feel logical. Both are quietly wrong. Let’s walk through why, without jargon, and then look at what a sensible setup actually looks like on an ordinary Windows PC.

What encryption actually does

Encryption scrambles your data using a key, so it’s unreadable to anyone who doesn’t have that key. The scrambling is done with maths, not obscurity. A modern cipher like AES-256 is designed so that guessing the key by brute force would take longer than the age of the universe on any hardware that exists or is likely to exist.

So when encryption is done properly, nobody “breaks” it. The attacker goes around it: they guess your password, find the key lying next to the lock, or take advantage of a file you forgot to protect. That single idea explains almost everything else in this article.

“I have nothing to hide”

Most people aren’t hiding anything illegal. But privacy was never only about secrets. Think about what’s sitting on your drive right now:

  • Scans of your Aadhaar, PAN, passport or driving licence
  • Bank statements and tax returns
  • Saved logins and browser sessions
  • Family photos, private chats, medical reports
  • Client files or work documents that aren’t yours to leak

You don’t need to be hiding something for those to be valuable. Identity documents are exactly what fraudsters want. Photos and messages are exactly what hurts when they get out. “Nothing to hide” really means “nothing I’d be happy to hand over”, and that’s a much shorter list than most of us admit.

“My Windows password protects my files”

This is the most common misunderstanding, so it’s worth being precise. Your Windows sign-in password protects the session: it decides who gets to the desktop. It does not scramble your files.

If someone removes your drive and plugs it into another computer, or boots from a USB stick, the sign-in screen is simply never part of the story. Your files are right there, readable, because nothing on the disk was encrypted.

A login screen is a door. Encryption is what makes the walls solid. A door alone doesn’t help much if someone can walk around the building.

“That’s for hackers and governments”

The realistic threats for most of us are far more boring:

  1. A lost or stolen laptop. The most common one, and the one encryption handles best.
  2. Someone with brief physical access. A shared PC, a repair shop, a relative, a curious colleague.
  3. Leaked or reused passwords. Billions of credentials from old breaches are floating around. If you reuse passwords, one leak can open many doors.
  4. Malware or cloud mishaps. Sync the wrong folder to the wrong place and plaintext copies are suddenly everywhere.

None of these need a movie-style hacker. They need an ordinary bad day. Encryption is the thing that stays quiet and works anyway.

Where encryption quietly fails

Here’s the part most “just encrypt everything!” articles skip. Encryption can be real and still useless in practice. The usual culprits:

FailureWhat it looks like
Weak passwordA strong cipher protected by Name@123 is as strong as Name@123.
Cheap key derivationYour password is turned into a key too quickly, so attackers can try billions of guesses per second.
Key stored beside the lockThe decryption key sits in a plain file or note on the same machine.
Forgotten plaintext copiesTemp files, “Downloads”, cloud-synced originals, and old backups.
No recovery planYou forget the password, and the data is gone for good. Which is exactly how it’s supposed to work.

The second row deserves a closer look, because it’s invisible to users. When you type a password, the software has to turn it into an actual encryption key. This step is called a key derivation function, and its job is to be deliberately slow and memory-hungry so that guessing becomes expensive. A modern choice here is Argon2id, the winner of the Password Hashing Competition. It forces every guess to burn real memory and time, which makes mass guessing on GPUs far less practical.

When I built ATLOCK, this was one of the first decisions I made. Its vault and protected files use Argon2id to derive keys and AES-256-GCM to seal the data. GCM matters because it’s authenticated: if even one byte of an encrypted file is tampered with, decryption fails loudly instead of silently handing you corrupted data. I mention it not to show off but because it’s a good test for any tool you consider: can it tell you plainly what it uses, and does it make sense?

A sensible setup for a normal person

You don’t need to encrypt your whole life. You need a few layers that cover the realistic risks:

1. Turn on full-disk encryption

This protects everything if the laptop is lost or stolen. On Windows 10/11 Pro that’s BitLocker; many Home editions offer “Device encryption” in Settings → Privacy & security. Check which one you have, and save the recovery key somewhere that isn’t the same laptop.

2. Stop reusing passwords

Use a password vault and let it generate a unique password per site. A vault that lives on your own device, with no account and no cloud sync, removes an entire category of risk. I wrote a separate walkthrough on that: How to keep an offline encrypted password vault on Windows.

3. Give your most sensitive folders their own lock

Full-disk encryption protects a powered-off laptop. It does very little once you’re signed in and walk away from your desk. A second layer for the genuinely sensitive stuff (ID scans, financial records, client data) means that someone who gets onto your unlocked PC still can’t open it. This is the job of a file-level lock like ATLOCK’s File Guard, and there’s a step-by-step in how to protect a folder in Windows.

4. Use a long passphrase, not a clever password

Four or five unrelated words beat P@ssw0rd! every time, and they’re easier to remember. If a tool offers a key file or hardware key as an extra factor on top of your password, even better.

5. Keep an encrypted backup, and test the recovery

Encryption without a backup is a trap. Encrypted backups without a tested recovery are the same trap with extra steps. Do one restore a year to be sure it works.

How to judge any encryption tool

Whether you pick BitLocker, VeraCrypt, KeePass, ATLOCK or something else, ask the same questions:

  • Does it name its algorithms? “Military-grade” is marketing. “AES-256-GCM with Argon2id” is information.
  • Does it work offline? Fewer network connections means fewer places for your data to leak.
  • Does it need an account? If your lock depends on someone else’s server, you’ve added a dependency you don’t control.
  • Is it honest about its limits? Good security software tells you what it cannot do.

That last one is worth a pause. Software running as you, on your PC, can’t fully defend against an attacker who already controls your account. Secure deletion on SSDs can’t be guaranteed. Full-disk encryption like BitLocker is still the right foundation. I’ve put these caveats in ATLOCK’s own documentation because pretending otherwise would be a worse kind of security. A tool that promises you “unbreakable” is a tool that hasn’t thought hard about the problem.

So, does encryption really matter?

Yes. Not because someone is hunting you, but because computers get lost, passwords get leaked, and people get curious. Encryption is cheap insurance against all three, and unlike most insurance, it costs you nothing when nothing goes wrong.

You don’t have to do everything today. Pick one: turn on disk encryption, move your passwords into a vault, or lock the one folder you’d hate to see leaked. Any one of those puts you ahead of most people.
